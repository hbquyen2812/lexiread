The obituaries were written early and often. By the middle of the 1970s the trade papers had agreed on a date by which the last commercial letterpress shop in Europe would close, and each year the estimate was pulled back towards the present. Setting type by hand was how nearly everything had been printed since the 1450s. From the 1880s much of it was cast mechanically, line by line, on a machine the size of a wardrobe. Within a single working lifetime it became how almost nothing was printed. The forecasts were right about the shops, which closed. They were wrong about the machinery. Most of the workshops in Europe and North America currently printing from metal opened after 2000.

Part of the reason the equipment went so quickly is that it was worth money. Type is an alloy of lead, tin and antimony, and a scrapyard would pay for it by weight. Whole composing rooms were sold for scrap within days of a firm deciding to convert.

The technology that displaced it was not simply cheaper. Photocomposition set text on film, from which an offset plate could be made in minutes. A job that took a compositor two days could be done overnight. Rosalind Cadby, a historian of printing at Kelverstone University, has followed what became of the compositors. Compositors were not, on the whole, retrained. They were offered redundancy. The firms that survived recruited typists instead. Cadby estimates the British losses at about forty thousand composing jobs.

Survival was largely accidental. A few printing museums and technical colleges took a press each, but the machines behind the present revival were rescued singly by private people: a compositor who paid scrap value for a hand press and put it in a garage, a teacher who hung on to a platen when the department gave it up, a printer's widow who could not face ringing the dealer. Weight decided a great deal. A hand press can be taken to pieces and moved a bit at a time, and a great many hand presses came through; a cylinder press weighs upwards of a tonne and a half, needs a crane to lift it and a floor that will take it, and very few were saved. Type is a separate difficulty, because it wears out. Every impression flattens the metal a little, and a font that has been through tens of thousands of impressions is no longer crisp. A shop that prints regularly must buy new type, and new type can be cast only from matrices, one small piece of metal for each letter. Lukas Cordes, whose foundry at Wendholm supplies workshops in more than twenty countries, bought his matrices in 1987 from a firm closing its casting department.

The revival did not begin with customers. It began in art schools, where a few presses had been kept because they showed students what a letter is. In the 1990s designers emerged from those departments having set type by hand and found that they minded about it. The internet turned that enthusiasm into a business. A workshop in a small town could now take orders for stationery from buyers three countries away, willing to wait a month for delivery.

The work that pays is small, personal and decorative. Wedding invitations come first by a long way. Behind them come wine labels, jackets for books printed by other means, cards for people who want to be remembered. Miyako Aihara, who has run the Marram Press since 2005, keeps a record of everything that comes through the door: weddings bring in rather more than half her turnover, and in twenty years she has printed exactly four books. Her customers ask for the one thing she was trained to avoid. A compositor of the 1950s worked for an impression so light that the sheet was marked but not dented, and a deep bite in the paper was evidence of carelessness. Aihara's customers want to feel the letters under a thumb. She sets the press harder than she was taught to. She charges by the number of colours, with the depth of impression priced separately.

Whether any of this amounts to a trade is disputed. Yasmin Amrani, an economist at the Torvane Business School, surveyed 140 letterpress workshops throughout Europe and North America. Printing generated less than half the income of the median business. The rest came from teaching, from renting studio time, from selling their own prints and, in many cases, from a salary earned elsewhere. A workshop of that shape, in her analysis, is not a printing business that also teaches. It is a teaching business with a press in it. She does not find the description unkind. She thinks only that it changes what the revival is evidence of.

Cadby's reply is that the objection assumes a continuity nobody has established. The trade that shut down printed timetables, forms and ledgers, and nobody was employed in it who had not served a six-year apprenticeship. The workshops open now print none of those things. Most of their proprietors learned what they needed in a fortnight and have improvised since. On her reading this is not a survival but a second and unrelated use of the same equipment, of the kind that turns a mill into flats. The comparison is imperfect, since flats do not need the machinery that was there before them.

What settles the question may be duller than either side would like. A platen press has perhaps forty moving parts, and the engineers who understand them are mostly over seventy. Cordes has trained one apprentice in fifteen years and expects no more. Matrices for the common faces are safe. The rest are not. Whether people go on wanting an invitation they can feel is a question nobody in the workshops answers. The presses will outlast the fashion, as they have outlasted several already.
